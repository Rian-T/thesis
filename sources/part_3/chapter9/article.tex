\section{Two Routes to Supervision}

Even with synthetic generation, we can produce only a limited number of complete
longitudinal eCRF examples. This is not enough to train the extractor alone. We
therefore use two stages of supervision. General biomedical text first teaches
the model to locate spans from natural-language type descriptions. A smaller
task-specific corpus then adapts it to the fields and longitudinal structure of
the eCRF. \Cref{fig:p3-supervision-routes} shows these two stages.

Both stages use Qwen3-235B with constrained JSON output. For generalist
supervision, the model annotates biomedical passages sampled from
\textit{biomed-fr-v2} (\Cref{chap:mcbio}) with mentions and natural-language type
descriptions. We call the resulting annotation corpus
\textit{biomed-fr-v2-entities}. For task-specific supervision, it transforms
lymphoma cases from Biomed-Enriched (\Cref{chap:quality}) into sequences of clinical documents, then
assigns values from these documents to fields of the lymphoma eCRF. All
annotations are silver because they are generated by a language model rather
than provided by human annotators.

\input{sources/part_3/figures/fig03_supervision_routes}


\subsection{Generalist Annotation of Public Text}
\label{sec:gliner-data}

For each passage, Qwen3-235B proposes entity mentions together with a
natural-language description of each type, following the prompt given in
\Cref{app:gliner-prompts}. We search for every proposed mention in the source
passage and remove those we cannot locate. Each retained annotation therefore
corresponds to a span that appears in the text. Documents with no remaining
mention are kept as negative examples.

\subsection{Longitudinal Record Generation}
\label{sec:lymphoma-dossiers}

Published cases summarize a patient trajectory in a single narrative, whereas
the target form is filled from several reports written at different points in
care. We start from the open clinical cases identified in Biomed-Enriched
(\Cref{chap:quality}). Qwen3-Next-80B-A3B selects those that describe lymphoma.
Each selected case receives a reproducible fictitious French identity, hospital,
city, and anchor year. \Cref{fig:p3-case-to-record} shows a source case and the
synthetic record generated from it.

\input{sources/part_3/figures/fig04_case_to_record}

Generation follows the clinical chronology. We first convert the source case into
a structured French timeline and harmonize its dates, ages, and bounded values.
Reports are then generated one at a time, and after each one we extract the
timeline again from the result as a consistency check. As shown in
\Cref{fig:p3-wavefront}, a report at time $t$ receives only the relevant events
that have already occurred.

\input{sources/part_3/figures/fig04_wavefront}

The generated reports keep the chronology of the source case but replace its
compact summary with documents written at distinct moments of care.
\Cref{app:lymphoma-dossier} shows one generated record in full.

Practising clinicians at French university hospitals (CHU) reviewed successive
versions of the synthetic records through a web annotation interface and
discussions. Their feedback concerned both the silver annotations and the realism
of the generated text, and informed later versions of the pipeline. This review
remained informal. A reproducible assessment with a defined protocol and a panel
of clinicians is left for future work.

The resulting corpus contains 2,050 longitudinal records, each with a median of
eight reports and 22,314 characters, and 31\% of them include a second treatment
line. We use 410 records for testing and 100 of the remaining 1,640 records for
validation, which leaves up to 1,540 records for training.


\subsubsection{The Silver Reference}
\label{sec:tapdl}

The generated reports are not yet an extraction reference. The lymphoma use case
defined within the OncoLab project specifies 89 variables of interest, which
form the fields of the eCRF used here. To create the reference, each relevant
value must be assigned to one of these fields and located in the complete patient
record. The assignment also depends on the clinical chronology, since the same
treatment may fill a first-line field before relapse and a second-line field
afterwards. We therefore label each report together with its date, document type,
and corresponding events.

For each patient, the language model reads the complete sequence of reports and
fills the 89 fields of the eCRF. For every non-empty field, it returns both a
predicted value and an excerpt from the record that supports this prediction.
The excerpt serves as textual evidence and does not have to be identical to the
predicted value, since the information may be expressed indirectly in the
record.

We validate each evidence excerpt by locating it in the patient record. We first
search for an exact match and use approximate matching if the exact search fails.
If neither method finds the excerpt, we set the corresponding field to null. A
successful match provides the character offsets of the evidence span used in the
silver reference. \Cref{fig:p3-tapdl} summarizes this process.

\input{sources/part_3/figures/fig05_tapdl}

The pipeline yields 237,194 field--evidence span assignments across the 89
variables. Every retained evidence span can be located in the source record, but
this does not guarantee that the predicted value or its field assignment is
medically correct.


\section{Distributional Comparison with Human Text}
\label{sec:stylometry}

The generated records can be internally consistent while remaining different
from human-written clinical reports. We use PARHAF~\citep{tannier_parhaf_2026},
a set of fictitious but human-written reports containing no hospital data, as
the closest available open reference for French clinical text. We compare it
with the raw clinical cases, our generated records, and published E3C clinical
cases~\citep{magnini_e3c_2021}.

For each source, we sample 1,000 texts and encode them with MC-bio-embed, the
biomedical encoder described in \Cref{chap:architectures}. We compare the
resulting distributions with PARHAF using MAUVE~\citep{pillutla_mauve_2021},
Fr\'echet embedding distance (FED)~\citep{heusel_fid_2017}, maximum mean
discrepancy (MMD)~\citep{gretton_kernel_2012}, and a classifier two-sample test
(C2ST)~\citep{lopezpaz_c2st_2017}. Higher MAUVE scores indicate greater
similarity, whereas lower FED and MMD scores do. C2ST measures how accurately a
classifier distinguishes two sources, with 0.5 corresponding to chance.

\begin{figure}[t]
  \centering
  \includegraphics[width=\textwidth]{plots/part_3/output/fig06_stylometry.pdf}
  \caption{Distributional comparison of three text sources with PARHAF. The
  metrics do not produce a consistent ranking, but C2ST shows that every source
  remains readily distinguishable from PARHAF.}
  \label{fig:p3-stylometry}
\end{figure}

The distance measures place the raw clinical cases closest to PARHAF, followed
by the generated records and E3C, whereas C2ST produces the reverse ranking.
The metrics therefore do not show that generation consistently brings the
public cases closer to PARHAF. Moreover, all C2ST scores lie between 0.985 and
0.991, far above the chance level of 0.5.

This result is difficult to interpret as a measure of clinical realism. The
generated records were designed with guidance from hospital clinicians, whereas
PARHAF is only the closest open reference available. Their separation may
therefore reflect limitations of the generation process, differences between
PARHAF and the documents expected in the target hospitals, or both. Evaluation
against private hospital records would be needed to distinguish these
explanations.

\section*{Conclusion}

With the supervision in place, the remaining question is architectural. A model
must learn general extraction from broad biomedical annotations, adapt to a
small set of lymphoma records, and accept new field descriptions at inference.
The next chapter develops a compact extractor for this setting.
