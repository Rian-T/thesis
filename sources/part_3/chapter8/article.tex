\section{Introduction}

A fixed classifier can only return the classes it saw during training. A model
trained on one set of clinical fields therefore cannot answer a question about a
field it has never seen. This chapter builds an extractor that reads clinical
text and returns the values of fields that were not fixed in advance, without
using any hospital label to learn them.

At inference, GLiNER~\citep{zaratiana2024gliner} receives each requested type
together with a natural-language description of what it means. It represents
candidate spans and type descriptions in a shared space, then selects the spans that match each
description. This design allows the same model to
extract new fields without replacing a fixed classification layer. Its usefulness
for medical text therefore depends on how well it represents medical
descriptions.


\section{The Open-Vocabulary Extractor}

Medical descriptions can name the same concept in different ways, while closely
related concepts may differ by only a few words. ModernCamemBERT-bio has learned
from biomedical text, but its pretraining objectives do not explicitly teach it
which descriptions should be close or far apart. We therefore add a contrastive
training stage to improve its representation of complete inputs, and call the
resulting model ModernCamemBERT-bio-embed, or MC-bio-embed.

We return to the CIM-10, ATC, and CCAM resources introduced in
\Cref{chap:ontobook}. OntoBook turned their relations into pretraining text.
Here, we use their labels, synonyms, definitions, and relations directly to
train representations of complete inputs. Positive pairs express matching or
related concepts, while exclusions and closely related concepts provide hard
negatives. MC-bio-embed represents each input by mean-pooling its token
representations and applying L2 normalization.

\input{sources/part_3/figures/fig07_medembed}

For contrastive training, we used a multiple-negatives ranking loss and a
triplet loss. The multiple-negatives ranking loss uses the other examples in the
batch as negative pairs. The triplet loss compares each positive pair with a
related but incorrect concept used as a hard negative. The full training
settings are reported in \Cref{tab:hparams}.

We also continued contrastive training on biomedical passages and complete
documents to improve the representation of long inputs. However, this second
training stage reduced retrieval performance, especially on FRACCO. We
therefore retained the checkpoint trained only on medical terminologies.

To test the resulting embeddings, we construct OntoBench-FR, a benchmark for
retrieving medical codes and relations from text. It contains 33,595
four-choice items across 22 tasks. CIM-10 contributes seven tasks,
ATC four, and CCAM seven. They cover label-to-code and synonym-to-code
retrieval, hierarchy, exclusions, causal and manifestation relations, and
associated or incompatible procedures. For example, given the synonym
``choléra classique'', the model must select its CIM-10 code, A00.0, among four
related codes.

FRACCO~\citep{pignat2024fracco} contributes four tasks built from French
oncology reports: span-to-code, context-to-code, document-to-code, and
code-to-span retrieval. For every item, the model encodes the question and four
candidates and selects the candidate with the highest similarity. We report
accuracy at rank one, averaged equally across tasks within each family and then
across the four families.

MC-bio-embed obtains the highest mean score and leads on CIM-10, CCAM, and
FRACCO, while Solon~\citep{arbaretier2024solon} remains slightly stronger on
ATC. The terminology results
mainly measure in-domain grounding because their source terminologies were used
during training. FRACCO provides stronger evidence of transfer, since its tasks
are built from clinical text.

Retrieving the right medical concept does not yet locate its mentions in a
document. These results lead us to retain MC-bio-embed, rather than
ModernCamemBERT-bio, as the backbone of the GLiNER2 extractor used
below~\citep{zaratiana2025gliner2}. We train the extractor on the annotations in
\textit{biomed-fr-v2-entities} (\Cref{sec:gliner-data}). The resulting
150-million-parameter model is called MC-bio-gliner.


\section{Public Clinical Text in the Training Corpus}

We train the extractor on three versions of
\textit{biomed-fr-v2-entities}. The first uses the complete corpus. The second
removes all clinical paragraphs. The third also removes them, but repeats
biomedical examples until the corpus has its original size. Comparing the last
two conditions measures the effect of adding more examples, while comparing the
first and third measures the effect of their clinical content.

We evaluate the three models zero-shot on the same 200 generated patient
records, before any adaptation to the lymphoma form. \Cref{fig:p3-section-supervision}
reports micro-averaged value-$F_1$, defined in \Cref{chap:evaluation}, globally
and for each section of the form.

\begin{figure}[t]
  \centering

  \caption{Value-$F_1$ by training-corpus composition, globally and by eCRF
  section. The size-matched biomedical control is reported only for the global
  score.}
  \label{fig:p3-section-supervision}
\end{figure}

The absolute scores remain low because the models have never been trained on
the lymphoma task. We therefore focus on differences between corpus
conditions. Value-$F_1$ reaches 0.205 with the complete corpus, compared with
0.183 after removing the clinical paragraphs, a difference of 0.022. Matching
the corpus size with repeated biomedical examples does not recover this loss
and instead gives 0.170. In this experiment, the gain therefore comes from the
clinical source rather than from the number of training examples alone.

The section scores show where this difference occurs. Without public clinical
text, value-$F_1$ falls from 0.211 to zero for first-line treatment and from
0.050 to zero for second-line treatment. By comparison, disease history changes
from 0.242 to 0.228. On these 200 generated records, public clinical text mainly
helps the extractor identify treatment information.


\section{Extraction with Additional Types}

We selected MC-bio-embed based on its results on OntoBench-FR, but we have not
yet tested whether it is also a better backbone for GLiNER2. We therefore
replace it with ModernCamemBERT-bio and leave everything else unchanged.

We evaluate both models on the twenty types listed in \Cref{tab:type-bank}. Ten
are common clinical types used in existing benchmarks, which we call benchmark
types. The other ten are additional compositional or cross-domain types not
used in these benchmarks. They may still appear in the training data, so this
experiment does not measure transfer to entirely unseen concepts.

Evaluation on \textit{biomed-fr-v2-entities} measures whether the extractor
recovers the spans originally annotated by Qwen3-235B. However, the extractor
may return a valid span that is absent from these annotations. We therefore
reverse the evaluation. We pool the spans returned by both models, remove
duplicates, and hide which model produced each span. Qwen3-235B then evaluates
whether each prediction matches the requested type. We use these judgements to
compute mean average precision (MAP) for each model. We call this metric
LLM-judge MAP.
\Cref{fig:p3-novel-types} reports the results for both sets of types.

For benchmark types, LLM-judge MAP increases from 0.478 with
ModernCamemBERT-bio to 0.517 with MC-bio-embed, a gain of 0.039. For
additional types, it increases from 0.333 to 0.382, a gain of
0.049. The slightly larger observed gain in the second condition is consistent
with the purpose of contrastive description training. It remains evidence from
an LLM judge rather than agreement with a human medical reference.


\section*{Conclusion}

At this stage, MC-bio-gliner is a generalist extractor. We ultimately want to
fill a lymphoma eCRF from a complete patient record. \Cref{chap:evaluation}
tests whether MC-bio-gliner can be adapted to this task.
